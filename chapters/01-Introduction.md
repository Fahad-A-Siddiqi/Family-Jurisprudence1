Introduction
A father leaves a Guardian Court in Lahore having just spent two hours with his son inside a courtroom annex, under the eye of a court official, for the first time in six weeks. He will be back next month for the same two hours. Nobody in that room today asked what this particular boy, at this particular age, actually needs from his father. Nobody wrote down why two hours, why once a month, why here rather than at the boy’s own home. The order simply says so, in language close to the order in the file before it, and the file before that. This book began, in a real sense, in rooms like that one — not in a library, and not as an abstract question about what Pakistani law should say about children, but as a practising lawyer’s accumulated discomfort with what it was already saying, in theory, and what was actually happening, in practice, to the families walking out of those courtrooms.
The gap between those two things is this book’s real subject. Pakistani custody law, examined as doctrine, is more sophisticated than its reputation. The welfare principle in section 17 of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 has been read by the Supreme Court and the High Courts, over decades, in ways that resist rigid status-based rules, take a child’s own relationships seriously, and refuse to let a mother’s remarriage or a father’s absence settle a case on its own. None of that sophistication was invented for this book. It is already there, in the reported judgments this book cites throughout. What is not there, or not consistently there, is the machinery that would let that doctrine reach the family in the room this introduction opened with — reliable fact-finding, reasoned interim orders, institutions built for the specific work of keeping a child connected to two households instead of one. This book’s central claim is that the distance between Pakistani custody doctrine and Pakistani custody practice is not a failure of the doctrine. It is a failure to build the institutions the doctrine already assumes.
What “continuing parenthood” means, and does not mean
The book’s argument starts from a distinction simple enough to state in a sentence and easy enough to lose sight of in practice: marriage ends, but parenthood, in the ordinary case, does not. A divorce or a separation reorganises a household. It does not, by itself, answer what a child’s relationship with each parent should now look like. Pakistani custody law has a long-standing vocabulary problem here. Once a court names one parent custodial, the language of the proceeding itself starts doing work the welfare principle never authorised — the other parent drifts, in the file, in the schedule, and eventually in the family’s own understanding, from parent to visitor. This book calls the underlying relationship, once residence has been decided but before every other question has been assumed to follow from it, continuing parenthood.
That phrase is not a claim to equal time, a presumption of shared custody, or an argument that every parent is entitled to unrestricted access regardless of risk. This book says so explicitly and more than once, because the alternative — treating continuing parenthood as a rhetorical trump card — would repeat the exact error this book spends its length diagnosing: letting one idea silently answer questions it was never built to answer. Where a child’s safety requires restriction, supervision, or in a serious case suspension, welfare requires exactly that, and nothing in this book argues otherwise. The claim is narrower and, this book has tried to show, more defensible: a decision about where a child lives is not automatically a decision about what the child’s relationship with the other parent is worth. Those are different questions, and Pakistani doctrine, read carefully, has never actually required them to be treated as one.
How the book is built
Each chapter in this book is built around a single question, stated in its own title, answered on its own terms, and closed with an explicit account of what that chapter adds that no earlier chapter has already said. That discipline was not adopted for tidiness. It was adopted because the opposite habit — a legal text that restates its own thesis under a new heading every few pages — produces exactly the kind of book this one is trying not to be: long on repetition, short on cumulative argument. Where two chapters risked answering the same question from slightly different angles, this book has merged them. Where a chapter’s opening line promised a question its body never actually answered, this book has cut it or rebuilt it. That process is ongoing rather than finished, and later editions of this book will likely tighten it further.
The chapters move in a broad arc rather than a flat list. The opening chapters state the book’s thesis and build the conceptual vocabulary — parenthood, custody, residence, guardianship, responsibility, contact — that later chapters depend on without having to redefine each time. A historical section then asks how custody, a concept originally about where a child sleeps, came to stand in for the whole of parenthood in ordinary legal and lay usage alike. A long doctrinal core follows, tracing what Pakistani welfare jurisprudence actually holds, how it interprets its own governing statutes, and how it has developed through decades of reported decisions. From there the book turns to institutions — courts, mediation, judicial training, the machinery that has to exist for doctrine to reach a family rather than stay in the law reports — and then to procedure, where delay, cost, evidentiary practice and the asymmetries between represented and unrepresented parties get the sustained attention this book thinks they deserve and have not always received. A section returns to the child directly, asking what welfare requires when it is understood as more than the avoidance of harm. A final section turns to reform, asking plainly what it would actually take, in statute and in institutional design, to close the gap this introduction opened with.
What this book is not
This book is not a policy manifesto for equal parenting time, and it takes that comparative debate on directly rather than avoiding it, stating the case for a shared-parenting presumption fairly before explaining why a properly resourced, case-by-case welfare standard remains preferable even where the two approaches often reach the same result. It is not a work of empirical social science, and it says so about itself more bluntly than most legal texts are willing to say about their own limits: everything in this book is built from reported judgments, and reported judgments are not a representative sample of what happens to the ordinary family in the ordinary Guardian Court. A later chapter names that gap directly rather than letting the book’s own confident tone imply an authority over lived outcomes this book has not earned and does not claim.
It is also not, despite where some of its material began, a work of advocacy for any one litigant or any one side of the recurring contest between custodial and non-custodial parents. Some of the material in this book’s later chapters is drawn from real litigation, including constitutional petitions filed in the course of ordinary practice, because that litigation documented patterns — an unwritten national template for interim visitation, a bailiff’s fee charged without statutory basis, a maintenance decree nobody could actually collect — that reported doctrine alone would never have surfaced. Where that material entered this book, it was rebuilt from argument into analysis: the underlying legal question kept, the advocate’s framing set aside, and every claim this book could not independently verify — a statistic without a citation, a diagnosis without clinical standing — left out rather than imported on the strength of a client’s brief. A footnote’s worth of honesty about where material comes from is worth more than a false appearance of having arrived at every proposition from the law reports alone.
Why this matters now
Pakistani family courts handle tens of thousands of custody and guardianship disputes a year, a number this book can state with more confidence than it can state almost anything else about outcomes, because pendency figures are collected in a way case-level outcome data simply is not. Behind each file is a child whose relationship with one parent is being organised, sometimes well, sometimes through a mechanically repeated order nobody has reconsidered in years. The welfare principle this book has traced through its doctrinal chapters is, on the evidence available, being applied thoughtfully wherever a case reaches the Supreme Court or a reported High Court judgment. Whether the same care reaches the family whose case never leaves the trial court is a question this book has been honest enough to say it cannot answer with the material available to it. What it can do, and has tried to do across the chapters that follow, is state as precisely as possible what Pakistani law already requires, where that requirement is not being met, and what would actually have to change — not as a matter of philosophy, but as a matter of statute, staffing and institutional design — for the answer to that unanswerable question to start looking better than it currently does.
